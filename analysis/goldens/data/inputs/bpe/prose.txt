Bread starts with flour, water, salt and yeast. The baker mixes them into a rough dough and lets it rest. While it rests, the flour soaks up the water and the gluten begins to form. Then the baker kneads the dough, folding and pressing it until it turns smooth and springy.

The yeast feeds on the sugars in the flour and gives off gas. The gas is caught in the gluten, and the dough rises. A slow rise in a cool room gives the bread more flavour than a fast rise in a warm one. Some bakers let the dough rise overnight in a cold place for that reason.

When the dough has doubled, the baker shapes it. A round loaf, a long loaf and a flat loaf all start from the same dough. The shaped loaves rise once more, and then they go into a hot oven. Steam in the oven keeps the crust soft at first, so the loaf can grow, and then the crust sets and browns.

A loaf is done when it sounds hollow when tapped on the bottom. It should cool before it is cut, because the inside is still baking as it cools. Cutting it too soon leaves the crumb gummy.

Bakers have made bread this way for thousands of years. The tools have changed, and the ovens have changed, but the steps are the same: mix, rest, knead, rise, shape, rise again and bake. Each step takes time, and each step matters. A baker who rushes one of them can taste it in the loaf.

Sourdough bread uses a starter in place of bought yeast. A starter is flour and water left to sour, fed every day with more flour and water. Wild yeasts and bacteria live in it. They raise the dough more slowly than bought yeast, and they give the bread its sour taste. A starter can be kept alive for years, and some bakers keep starters that are older than they are.
